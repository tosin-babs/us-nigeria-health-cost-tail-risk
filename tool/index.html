Catastrophic health spending is usually compared between countries with a single average. An average counts how many households cross a line; it says nothing about how far past it the worst-affected go. This page puts the whole distribution on view, and shows why a tail comparison is only as good as the way its denominator is built.

How heavy is each tail, and does the answer depend on the denominator?

ξ is the shape of a generalized Pareto distribution fitted to the worst 10% of each distribution. Above zero the tail is heavy; above 0.5 the variance is infinite. Dividing Nigerian spending by a consumption aggregate that already contains it (the SDG basis) bounds the ratio below one and makes the Nigerian tail look light. On every construction that treats the two countries alike, the two tail indices are similar and both are heavy. Intervals are from a bootstrap over sampling units within strata; p is for the US minus Nigeria difference.

What the comparison can and cannot say

Reading the two distributions

Nigeria's burden is higher at every percentile from the median up, on every construction of the denominator. Both countries' out-of-pocket distributions are heavy-tailed, with a tail index near 0.5 whether the tail is measured on the ratio or on spending in international dollars. An earlier version of this page reported that the American tail was heavy and the Nigerian one bounded. That contrast came from dividing Nigerian spending by a consumption aggregate that contains it, which caps the ratio at one; it disappears once both denominators are built the same way.

The denominators are not the same variable. The American survey records income and no consumption; the Nigerian one records consumption and unreliable income. Neither matched construction is exact, so a conclusion is only kept here if it holds on both.

Neither survey sees forgone care. A household that went without treatment appears as a zero. In the Nigerian survey, 4% of ill members in the poorest fifth consulted no one because of cost, against 1% in the richest fifth. In the American survey, over half of the families whose out-of-pocket spending exceeded a year's income carried medical debt.

This is not a personal risk assessment. Placing your ratio on a distribution tells you where that ratio sits among households surveyed in 2019–2024 and 2023/24. It is not a prediction about you.
